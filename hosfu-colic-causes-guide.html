Why Your Baby Cries, Gulps, and Arches After Every Feed

A calm, plain-English look at what colic actually is, the real reasons feeds go sideways, and the small changes that tend to make the biggest difference — including the one most parents overlook.

EH
Dr. Emily Hartman, RD
Pediatric feeding & nutrition specialist · Reviewed July 09, 2026
Colic is common, exhausting — and more manageable than it feels at 3am

If your evenings have turned into a blur of inconsolable crying, clenched fists, and a baby who pulls away mid-bottle, you are not doing anything wrong — and you are very far from alone. Roughly one in five babies goes through a stretch of colic. Here's what's actually happening inside those feeds, and the changes that tend to help most.

1

First — what colic actually is (and isn't)

Colic isn't a disease or something you'll find on a lab report. It's a pattern. Pediatricians describe it with the "rule of three": crying for more than three hours a day, more than three days a week, for more than three weeks — in a baby who is otherwise healthy, feeding, and growing.

It usually begins around two to three weeks of age, peaks near six weeks, and eases off by three to four months. That timeline won't stop tonight's crying, but it does mean there is a finish line, even when it doesn't feel like one.

What's really going on

Five things that quietly make feeds harder

Colic rarely has one single cause. But when a baby gulps, arches, and cries after eating, it usually traces back to some mix of these five — and most of them are easier to influence than they look.

Cause 01

Swallowed air

A fast or poorly-sealed latch means baby takes in nearly as much air as milk. That trapped air is what causes the arching, squirming, and pulling away mid-feed.

Cause 02

Milk flowing too fast

A nipple that flows faster than a baby can pace themselves forces gulping, choking, and extra swallowed air — the opposite of a calm, steady feed.

Cause 03

An immature gut

A newborn's digestive system is still learning to move milk and gas along. Some fussiness is simply development — not something you did wrong.

Cause 04

Feeding too fast or too much

Big, quick feeds overwhelm a small stomach — and what doesn't fit tends to come straight back up, often with tears attached.

Cause 05

Reflux

When the muscle at the top of the stomach is still weak, milk — and discomfort — rises back up after feeds, leaving baby unsettled and sore.

2

What actually helps at feed time

You can't fully switch off a baby's colic — but you can lower the amount of air and pressure going in, and that's where most of the relief comes from. A few small habits, done consistently, tend to add up faster than any single "fix."

  • ✓Feed paced and upright. Hold baby at roughly a 45° angle and let them take breaks instead of draining the bottle in one go.
  • ✓Burp early and often. Pause halfway through the feed, not only at the end, to clear air before it builds.
  • ✓Smaller, more frequent feeds. A less-full tummy is a calmer tummy — and less likely to send milk back up.
  • ✓Stay upright after. Twenty to thirty minutes vertical helps milk settle instead of rising.
  • ✓Cut the air at the source. Choose a bottle that lets air escape rather than forcing it back into your baby.
3

The part most parents overlook: the bottle itself

Technique does a lot of the work — but the bottle in your hand either helps you or fights you. Most of the air a bottle-fed baby swallows comes from two places: a nipple that can't hold a steady latch, and a vent system that pushes air back through the milk instead of letting it out.

Older anti-colic bottles try to fix this with a maze of tubes and valves. They can work — when they're spotless — but they trap milk and are miserable to clean. Important

When it's more than colic — call your pediatrician

Colic is, by definition, crying in an otherwise healthy baby. If any of the following show up, it isn't colic — it's a reason to get checked. Your instinct to ask is always valid.

  • Fever, or forceful / green vomiting
  • Blood in the stool or spit-up
  • Diarrhea or signs of dehydration
  • Poor weight gain or refusing to feed
  • Crying that sounds weak, high-pitched, or "different"
  • Pain and arching that continues well past 4 months

This article is general parenting information, not a substitute for medical advice. When something feels off, trust it and speak with your pediatrician about your baby.
